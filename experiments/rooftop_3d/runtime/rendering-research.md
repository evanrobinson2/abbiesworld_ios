# Bringing the illustrated rooftop to life on iPad

The gap is a coordinated art and rendering pipeline. I optimized an architectural reconstruction before establishing its visual character. My reading of the reference is a luminous garden room: readable lavender shadows, peach light filling the nook, soft blossom masses and a tree that frames the view. Our version has a dominant dark trunk, rigid furniture, isolated lights and a sparse canopy. More polygons or a bloom slider alone would not correct those choices.

An image generator solves a different problem. It can suggest thousands of blossoms, intricate bark and beautiful bounced light in one finished image without delivering their geometry, materials or hidden surfaces. Stable Diffusion’s underlying research concerns image synthesis; a real-time game needs a scene that remains coherent as the camera and foliage move, within roughly 33 milliseconds per frame at 30 fps. Generated artwork is therefore an excellent visual target and asset source, but it is not a complete rendering recipe. This comparison does not assume that Midjourney uses Stable Diffusion internally. [Latent diffusion paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2112.10752)

The lighting shortcut I took is a central problem. The current game copy uses unlit materials carrying baked illumination. Static shade is present, but moving branches cannot cast changing shadows onto those surfaces, and the bark cannot develop changing highlights. Small glow sprites also do not reproduce bright light spreading through the image. For this room, I recommend a hybrid: baked indirect light for the sheltered interior, light-responsive materials, one shadow-casting sun, restrained warm fill and selected moving foliage shadows. The interior should stay visibly pink and lavender under the roof. HDR lighting, controlled bloom and color grading then finish the image; they should not conceal an unreadable room. RealityKit already exposes environmental lighting and dynamic-light shadow controls. [Apple lighting APIs](https://developer.apple.com/documentation/realitykit/scene-content-lights-and-cameras)

The blossoms need purpose-built game assets. Mature vegetation workflows combine a few detailed foreground flowers with textured clusters on small curved meshes farther away. These form a dense, rounded canopy without individually modeling every petal. Branches need convincing taper, curved junctions and deliberate silhouettes. SpeedTree explicitly provides leaf-card geometry, detail levels and edited normals that make clusters light like fuller volumes. Our generic mesh reduction was the wrong tool for preserving that appearance. [SpeedTree card and lighting controls](https://docs9.speedtree.com/modeler/doku.php?id=card_generator)

Two additional techniques matter. First, thin petals need a cheap approximation of light passing through them: warm backlighting and softened shading across both sides. Epic documents the nearly black undersides produced when ordinary surface lighting is used for foliage without transmission. Second, blossom edges must remain stable as the camera moves. Test alpha-cutout cluster textures with multisample antialiasing and alpha-to-coverage where the renderer supports it; preserve opacity coverage in smaller mipmaps so the canopy does not disappear with distance. Trim empty card space and limit overlap, because transparent pixel work can dominate mobile cost. These are established production techniques, though their exact implementation must fit our renderer. [Epic foliage shading](https://dev.epicgames.com/documentation/unreal-engine/shading-models-in-unreal-engine), [Unity alpha-to-coverage](https://docs.unity3d.com/6000.0/Documentation/Manual/SL-AlphaToMask.html), [coverage-preserving mipmaps](https://docs.unity3d.com/6000.0/Documentation/ScriptReference/TextureImporter-mipMapsPreserveCoverage.html)

Wind can remain inexpensive. A vertex shader can bend branch tips and flutter blossom clusters using a shared breeze, per-cluster phase and a bend-strength mask; the separate pool of drifting petals needs no physical simulation. RealityKit supports both custom surface shading and vertex-position modifiers. Its newer instancing and custom full-screen post-processing are useful too, but the installed SDK marks those newer APIs for iPadOS 26, whereas this project targets 18.5. Metal supports alpha-to-coverage; that does not establish that RealityKit exposes the required switch. Those compatibility questions need a focused prototype before we promise the final effect. [Apple custom materials](https://developer.apple.com/documentation/realitykit/modifying-realitykit-rendering-using-custom-materials), [RealityKit instancing and post-processing](https://developer.apple.com/videos/play/wwdc2025/287/), [Metal coverage control](https://developer.apple.com/documentation/metal/mtlrenderpipelinedescriptor/isalphatocoverageenabled)

My recommended path is three milestones. First, finish one camera-matched corner—window, couch, bark and an overhanging blossom cluster—until its still image has the reference’s welcoming light and softness. Second, animate that corner and prove stable edges, light through petals and moving shadows during a slight camera turn. Third, expand the successful treatment across the scene and profile GPU time, memory and sustained thermals on the oldest supported iPad. Stay with RealityKit for that first proof; consider a Unity URP prototype only if a specific essential rendering control is unavailable. Triangle counts and simulator timing alone cannot approve the result. Apple’s profiling guidance specifically examines GPU work such as overdraw and bandwidth. [Apple GPU profiling](https://developer.apple.com/videos/play/wwdc2020/10603/)

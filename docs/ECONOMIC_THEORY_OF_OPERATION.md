# Economic Theory of Operation

The business is built around a simple economic principle:

**Customers should pay in proportion to the computational resources they actually consume, while the company earns a transparent margin for providing the product, infrastructure, orchestration, reliability, and experience around that computation.**

The product does not depend on traditional SaaS pricing logic. We do not need to artificially divide customers into feature tiers, restrict functionality to manufacture upgrade pressure, or guess in advance how much value a particular user will extract from the system.

Instead, the economic system follows the underlying cost structure of generative AI itself.

## 1. Credits Represent Computational Consumption

Credits are the common economic unit of the product.

A user purchases credits and spends them whenever an action causes the system to incur meaningful inference cost.

Examples may include:

* generating an image;
* creating a 3D asset;
* generating video or animation;
* synthesizing music or speech;
* running a sufficiently expensive language-model operation;
* performing future forms of generative computation.

Ordinary interaction with the product does not inherently require credits. Browsing, playing with previously generated content, organizing assets, navigating the world, and other low-marginal-cost activities can remain free or be governed separately.

The governing rule is:

**Inference creates cost. Inference consumes credits.**

This creates a direct relationship between customer activity, company cost, and company revenue.

## 2. Different Creations Have Different Prices

Not all generative work costs the same amount to perform.

A small language-model request may cost fractions of a cent. An image may cost substantially more. A high-resolution video, 3D reconstruction, or multi-stage generation pipeline may cost considerably more again.

The system therefore does not pretend that every creation has equal economic weight.

Each operation has a credit cost based substantially on its expected underlying variable cost.

Conceptually:

$$
\text{Credit Cost} = \text{Expected Variable Cost} + \text{Company Margin}
$$

The product should make these differences visible rather than obscure them.

A customer should be able to understand, at an appropriate level of detail, why one operation costs more than another.

Transparency does not mean every user must understand GPU economics or model-token pricing. It means that pricing should be explainable, predictable, and non-arbitrary.

For an adult, the explanation may be:

> This generation costs more because it uses a more computationally intensive model.

For a child, the exact same economic truth might simply appear as:

> This creation takes 8 credits because it uses more magic to make.

The abstraction changes. The underlying economics do not.

## 3. The Company Earns a Margin on Computation

The company does not attempt to hide the relationship between its costs and customer pricing.

Our economic role is to transform raw inference into a usable product.

That transformation includes:

* model selection;
* prompt orchestration;
* infrastructure;
* authentication;
* content storage;
* retries and failure handling;
* safety systems;
* asset processing;
* product design;
* workflow automation;
* customer support;
* payment processing;
* integration between different generation systems;
* ongoing product development.

The company therefore charges a margin above its underlying variable cost.

A simplified model is:

$$
P = C(1 + M)
$$

where:

* \(C\) is the relevant underlying cost;
* \(M\) is the markup;
* \(P\) is the customer price.

The precise cost basis may eventually include more than the invoice received from the model provider. As the system matures, the economically correct basis is likely to be **fully loaded variable cost** rather than raw inference alone.

That may include predictable costs associated with generation such as processing, storage, bandwidth, moderation, retries, and payment fees.

The philosophical commitment, however, remains unchanged:

**Our margin should be understandable and economically defensible.**

## 4. Margin Reflects Risk

The appropriate markup is not necessarily identical for every customer.

One of the principal risks the company assumes is uncertainty of demand.

A customer who purchases computation only when needed gives the company relatively little certainty regarding future revenue or infrastructure utilization.

A customer who prepays, commits to a larger amount of usage, or otherwise provides greater certainty reduces that risk.

Therefore:

**The greater the certainty of customer spend, the lower the required markup can be.**

This produces a rational discount structure without requiring arbitrary product tiers.

For example:

$$
\text{On-demand usage} \rightarrow \text{higher markup}
$$

$$
\text{prepaid usage} \rightarrow \text{lower markup}
$$

$$
\text{committed recurring usage} \rightarrow \text{lower markup still}
$$

The customer is not receiving a discount because they have been assigned to a prestigious pricing tier.

They are receiving a discount because they have changed the economics of the relationship.

They have transferred some demand risk away from the company.

That is the economic basis for the discount.

## 5. Features Are Not the Primary Pricing Lever

Traditional SaaS companies often create tiers such as Free, Pro, Business, and Enterprise and then selectively place features behind each tier.

That model frequently causes price to become disconnected from marginal cost.

Our model should resist that tendency.

Where practical, users should have access to the same fundamental creative capabilities.

What differs is how much computation they consume and how much economic certainty they provide.

The governing principle is:

**Charge for scarce resources rather than artificially restricting abundant functionality.**

This does not prohibit subscriptions, memberships, enterprise contracts, or bundled offerings.

It means those structures should ultimately reconcile to the same underlying economic system rather than becoming independent systems of arbitrary feature taxation.

## 6. Pricing Should Remain Legible

A credit system only works if customers retain an intuitive sense of what credits mean.

The company should therefore avoid unnecessary complexity such as:

* multiple incompatible credit currencies;
* hidden multipliers;
* unexplained surcharges;
* deliberately confusing expiration rules;
* unexpected deductions;
* invisible inference charges;
* artificial scarcity designed solely to encourage spending.

Before an inference operation begins, the user should ordinarily be able to see what it will cost.

A useful product principle is:

**Nothing silently consumes credits.**

For children, the presentation should be extremely simple.

For parents or account owners, the system can expose greater detail, including balances, history, generation type, and potentially the economic basis for different credit prices.

This creates two interfaces to the same accounting system:

**simple for the child, transparent for the adult.**

## 7. Model Providers Are Inputs, Not the Business Model

The company should remain economically agnostic regarding model provider.

OpenAI, Anthropic, Google, Meta, specialized image models, video models, 3D models, and future inference systems are suppliers of computational capability.

They are analogous to underlying infrastructure.

If a better model becomes available, the company should be free to change providers without redesigning its entire business model.

The credit abstraction enables this.

A particular operation might initially use a model costing $0.40 and later move to a superior model costing $0.12.

The product experience can remain unchanged while the economics improve.

Likewise, customers may elect to use a more expensive model when the quality difference warrants it.

The business therefore captures value by continuously optimizing the relationship among:

$$
\text{Quality} \times \text{Cost} \times \text{Latency} \times \text{Reliability}
$$

rather than becoming dependent on any individual model vendor.

## 8. Falling Inference Costs Should Be an Opportunity

Generative inference costs are likely to change substantially over time.

The company should not structure itself around the assumption that today's inference prices are permanent.

If inference becomes dramatically cheaper, several economically rational outcomes are possible:

* credit prices fall;
* users receive more generation for the same amount of money;
* higher-quality models replace cheaper models;
* previously uneconomic product experiences become viable;
* company margins expand temporarily;
* some combination of these occurs.

The company should retain discretion over pricing while maintaining its commitment to transparency.

We should reserve the right to alter pricing when underlying economics change materially, while clearly communicating significant changes and giving customers reasonable options when appropriate.

The promise is not that prices can never change.

The promise is that **pricing will not be deliberately inscrutable.**

## 9. The Company Makes More Money When Customers Create More Value

The model creates unusually strong alignment between company and customer.

The company benefits when users generate more things.

But users should only continue generating if those creations are useful, entertaining, meaningful, or delightful.

Revenue growth therefore depends substantially on creating reasons for customers to want more computation.

That is fundamentally healthier than relying on lock-in, unused subscriptions, or confusing contractual escalation.

The loop is:

$$
\text{Better product}
\rightarrow
\text{more creation}
\rightarrow
\text{more inference}
\rightarrow
\text{more revenue}
$$

The company's incentive is consequently to improve the usefulness and desirability of generation.

## 10. Unused Capacity Is Not the Product

Traditional subscription businesses can profit substantially from customers paying for services they rarely use.

That is not the core thesis here.

The desired behavior is actual engagement.

A customer generating thousands of assets should be economically valuable because they are actively using the system, not because they forgot to cancel a subscription.

This distinction matters culturally.

Our objective should be to build a business in which revenue is evidence of product utilization.

## 11. Credits Also Create an Internal Economic Language

Credits are not merely a payment mechanism.

They create a common economic language for the product organization.

Every proposed generative feature can be evaluated using questions such as:

* What does this operation actually cost?
* What should its credit price be?
* How frequently will users invoke it?
* How much value will users perceive relative to its cost?
* Can a cheaper model achieve the same result?
* Can caching or reuse eliminate unnecessary inference?
* Can several model calls be collapsed into one?
* Is the product encouraging wasteful generation?
* Does the experience justify the computational expense?

This makes unit economics visible at the product-design level rather than only in financial reporting after the fact.

Engineers, designers, and product managers can therefore make economically informed decisions continuously.

## 12. Efficiency Benefits Both the Company and the Customer

Because pricing derives from computational consumption, improving efficiency creates real value.

If an operation that once costs $1.00 can be redesigned to cost $0.20 while maintaining quality, the company creates economic surplus.

That surplus can be divided among:

* lower customer prices;
* higher company margin;
* improved output quality;
* additional generations;
* investment in new functionality.

There is therefore a permanent incentive to optimize inference rather than merely pass escalating costs through to users.

Model routing, caching, batching, prompt optimization, local computation, smaller models, and specialized models all become meaningful sources of competitive advantage.

## 13. Credits Should Feel Like Creative Energy, Not Gambling Currency

Because the product may be used by children, the credit system carries an additional obligation.

It should facilitate understandable resource consumption without creating manipulative spending mechanics.

The company should avoid deliberately exploiting:

* variable rewards;
* artificial urgency;
* opaque odds;
* accidental purchases;
* confusing currency conversions;
* psychologically manipulative depletion mechanics.

Credits exist primarily because generation has real economic cost.

They should feel like **creative energy**: something consumed to make something new.

The parent should retain meaningful visibility and control over expenditure.

The child should be able to understand whether an action is cheap, moderate, or expensive without needing to understand money.

## 14. The Long-Term Business Is an Economic Layer Over Machine Creativity

At sufficient scale, the company's fundamental economic function becomes broader than selling individual AI generations.

It provides an economic abstraction over computational creativity.

Customers express intent:

> Make me this.

The platform determines:

* which model or models should perform the work;
* how much computation is required;
* how to orchestrate those models;
* whether existing assets can be reused;
* what safety or quality processing is necessary;
* what the operation should cost;
* how the result becomes useful inside the product.

Credits provide a stable interface between the customer and a rapidly changing computational marketplace.

The models underneath can change continuously.

The customer's economic relationship with the product does not have to.

## Operating Doctrine

The business can therefore be summarized in a small number of rules:

1. **Inference consumes credits.**
2. **Credit costs reflect real differences in computational expense.**
3. **Pricing is transparent and explainable.**
4. **The company earns a stated or principled margin for providing the surrounding service.**
5. **Greater certainty of customer spend permits lower markup.**
6. **Features should not be artificially restricted merely to manufacture pricing tiers.**
7. **Customers see the cost before meaningful inference occurs.**
8. **Children receive simplicity; account owners receive transparency and control.**
9. **The platform continuously seeks cheaper and better ways to perform the same creative work.**
10. **Revenue should grow because customers want to create more, not because pricing has become harder to understand.**

The result is an economic system in which the customer's bill, the company's revenue, and the underlying cost of computation remain closely connected.

That simplicity is not merely a pricing strategy.

It is the operating theory of the business.

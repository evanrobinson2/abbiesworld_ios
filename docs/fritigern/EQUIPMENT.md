# Fritigern's world: equipment and dress reference (376 to 378 AD)

A short, plain-language sheet to settle the look before any soldiers are multiplied. It covers the Roman frontier army on the lower Danube and the Gothic (Tervingi) refugees who crossed in 376, from the crossing through Marcianople to Adrianople (9 August 378).

The rule of thumb: **this is not the Rome of Caesar or Trajan.** No crested legionary helmets, no segmented plate armour, no big curved rectangular shields, no bare legs. By the 370s the Roman soldier looks closer to an early medieval soldier than to a classical legionary.

## Roman soldiers

| Item | What it looked like in the 370s | What to avoid |
|---|---|---|
| Helmet | **Ridge helmet.** Two iron bowl halves joined by a strip running front to back over the top (the "ridge"). Separate cheek pieces tied on with leather, a small neck guard, often a nose guard on the better ones. Common type: **Intercisa** (simple, for infantry). Officers' type: **Berkasovo** (heavier, four-piece bowl, brow band, often sheathed in gilded silver and set with glass "gems"). | Horsehair crests, Corinthian or Imperial-Gallic helmets with big peaks and brow ridges. A small metal fin along the ridge is fine. |
| Body armour | **Mail** (most common) or **scale**, to mid-thigh, short or elbow sleeves. Many ordinary soldiers had none. | Segmented plate (the "lorica segmentata" look). It had gone out of use by about 250. |
| Shield | **Large oval or round**, flat or slightly dished, about 1 m tall, with a central iron boss. Painted with the unit's own design (rings, wreaths, stars, animals, Chi-Rho in some units). | Tall rectangular curved shields (scuta). |
| Clothing | **Long-sleeved wool tunic** to the knee, often off-white or undyed, decorated with woven stripes and roundels (clavi and orbiculi) in red, purple or dark blue. **Trousers** and closed leather boots. A **cloak** pinned at the right shoulder. | Short-sleeved tunics with bare legs and sandals. |
| Belt | A **wide military belt** with chip-carved bronze fittings. It was the badge of being a soldier or official. Officers wore a **crossbow brooch** (a big T-shaped fibula) on the cloak. | |
| Weapons | **Spear**, long **sword** (spatha), lead-weighted throwing darts (plumbatae), bows. | The short gladius as the main sword. |

**The officer whose helmet becomes the story object** (Lupicinus's guard at Marcianople, for example) would plausibly wear a **Berkasovo-type** helmet: gilded, gem-studded, a clear status object. That makes it readable from across a camp and memorable in a child's hands.

## Goths (Tervingi refugees)

| Item | What it looked like | Notes for the story |
|---|---|---|
| Clothing | Wool **tunic** and **trousers**, a **cloak** pinned with a bronze or silver **bow brooch**. Belts with a single buckle. Undyed wool, browns, madder red, woad blue. Men wore their hair long. | Families, not an army: lots of children, old people, livestock. |
| Women's dress | A long tube-shaped gown held by a **pair of brooches** at the shoulders, a belt, sometimes a headscarf. | |
| Armour | **Mostly none.** A few leaders in mail. Helmets were rare. | A Goth in Roman mail or a Roman helmet is visibly wearing loot. That's a story signal. |
| Shields | **Round**, plank-built, with a boss, plain or simply painted. | Smaller and plainer than the Roman oval. |
| Weapons | **Spears** and **javelins**, axes, bows; swords were for the wealthy. | They were supposed to give up their weapons at the crossing. Ancient sources say many bribed their way through, and after Marcianople they took Roman arms from the dead. "Where do the stolen weapons enter camp" is a real question. |
| Wagons | Four-wheeled **ox wagons**, often covered with hide or wool cloth on hoops. Drawn into a defensive **wagon circle** (the Romans called it *carrago*) when camped. | The wagon circle is the camp edge in the first scene. |

## Places in the first sequence

- **The camp**: a refugee camp of thousands outside a Roman town (Marcianople), short of food. Wagons, cooking fires, cattle, few tents.
- **The town wall and gate**: late Roman walls are thick, with **projecting towers** (square or U-shaped) either side of an arched gate. Gates were closed to the Goths; only leaders were let in for the banquet.
- **The banquet**: inside, in the commander's quarters. That's a later scene.

## How it carries into papercraft

Each item maps onto a grammar role, so the look comes from rules, not one-off models:

- **Ridge helmet**: two *faceted shells* (bowl halves) plus a *hemmed strip* (the ridge), *plates* for the cheek pieces and neck guard. A Berkasovo version adds a brow band and gem dots.
- **Mail and scale**: *plates* rule with many small shingled plates, low fold. Mail reads as a darker grey card with a fine texture; scale as overlapping rows.
- **Tunic, trousers, cloak**: *pleated sheets* with a centre fold per pleat. Roundels and stripes are cut-paper appliqué.
- **Oval shield**: one flat *stiffened card* with a painted cut-paper design and a cone boss.
- **Wagon**: *planks* (stiffened strips), spoked wheels, and a *pleated sheet* over hoops.

## Dramatic licence to keep in mind

- History doesn't record Alaric's father. Making Alaric Fritigern's son is a story choice, and a good one, but it's ours. Alaric was born around 370 to 375, so a small child in a wagon in 376 fits.
- Ammianus says Fritigern escaped the Marcianople banquet by persuading Lupicinus to let him calm his people. He doesn't say he was wounded. A wounded return is our invention too.

Main sources: Ammianus Marcellinus, *History*, book 31; the *Notitia Dignitatum* (shield designs); finds from Intercisa, Berkasovo and Deurne (helmets).

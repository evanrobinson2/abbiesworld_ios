What makes a great game HUD

Ten games, five ways to show information, and a measurable rubric, applied to the Ranch. The short answer is that the best HUD is the one a child stops seeing: three toys on the table, and everything else on the thing it is about.

Short version

Eight things every great HUD does

  1. You stop seeing it. Either the information moves into the world, or the overlay is so small and so fixed that the eye learns it and stops looking.
  2. It sits where the eyes already are. A thirsty cow shows it on the cow. Corners are for your own body and for time.
  3. Three things always on, at most. Don't Starve shows three needs and a clock. Everything else is summoned or appears on change.
  4. Meters have bodies. Don't Starve's sanity bends the screen; SteamWorld Dig's lamp shrinks the light. The number tells you and the world makes you feel it.
  5. Change is the signal. An element earns attention when it changes, then settles. Static elements are the quietest things on screen.
  6. Fades follow rules. Show on change, hold, fade; drop away while you hold something; one gesture brings it all back.
  7. Touch is its own design. No hover, fingers cover things, thumbs live at the bottom, and the notch and home strip are off limits.
  8. It is drawn in the world's own hand. Ink for Don't Starve, pencil for This War of Mine, the suit's lights for Dead Space. For us, toys.
Taxonomy

Five ways to say "the cow is thirsty"

The standard sort (Fagerholt and Lorentzon, 2009) asks two questions: would a character in the world see it, and does it sit in the world's space? That gives four kinds. We add a fifth, none, where the world says it on its own. Same cow, same thirst, five presentations.

Hierarchy

Always on, on the thing, or summoned

Every piece of information gets one home. It goes always-on only if the child needs it every few seconds and it changes what they do next. Score fails that test, so it lives on the café sign. Time passes it.

Touch versus mouse

Fingers cover what they touch

Move or drag over the phone. With a finger, the held card rides 60 px above the touch so the child can see where it lands; with a mouse it sits under the cursor and a hover preview is allowed. Green is the thumb zone, hatched strips are where iOS owns the screen (notch, home strip, back-swipe edge).

  • 64 px targets for children; 44 pt is Apple's absolute floor.
  • Nothing essential behind hover. Hold or drag gives the same preview.
  • Frequent targets in the bottom 40% in portrait. The sun dial is watched more than touched, so it can sit at the top.
  • iOS Safari has no vibration API, so sound and motion carry the feel.
Rubric

Seven gates, then 100 points

Gates (any failure rejects)

  1. Always-on HUD covers 8% of the screen or less, both orientations.
  2. Zero words in play mode; numbers are fine.
  3. Touch targets 64 px or more, inside the safe area.
  4. Nothing only on hover.
  5. Numerals 18 px cap height and 3:1 contrast over day, dusk and night.
  6. No game's UI art, icons, fonts or layout copied.
  7. Kid-safe: no red alarms, buzzers, fast flashing or fail screens.

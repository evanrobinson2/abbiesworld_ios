The City

A market town of a thousand real people on three hexes. It reads as a city because of its structure and its shared day, and every person in it has a home, a job and a plan.

The town is founded by simulation, not drawn

Land first (hills, two rivers that meet, the sea), then a founding party choosing the safe place that costs the least walking, then year-by-year growth. Streets, the bridge, mill, market and bell tower appear where the simulation measures the need.

What exists today

A diorama of 19 people, and the parts for a real town

The tray already has a city: Futurama, honestly labelled "traffic is decorative, not a city economy". It brings a three-hex footprint, district roads, palette recolouring and a parcel courier loop. Town life brings a household economy and job silhouettes (a hat and a prop per role). The shared resource loop, the fixed clock, the render pool, the holodeck and the chronograph are all ready to carry a city. The City builds on them, takes Futurama's tray slot when it is better, and keeps Futurama as a retro-future style variant.

1 · First principles

A thousand people, honestly

One square per person. 470 work (70 of them on neighbouring tiles), 150 children at school, 50 little ones, 180 elders, 150 adults caring, studying or between jobs.

One hex would need 600 people per hectare, which means tower blocks and a different game. Three Ranch-sized hexes give about 5 hectares, so 1,000 people sit at about 200 per hectare, the density of a historic walled town. The population is real, so the town never has to fake it with a counter.

Places of this size, for mood (populations approximate): Tobermory (about 1,000, a painted harbour front), Hallstatt (about 750, houses stacked between lake and mountain), Bibury (about 600, one stream and a row of cottages), Ogimachi in Shirakawa-go (about 600), the Cinque Terre villages. Everyone knows everyone by sight: about seven overlapping circles of 150 friends.

Five things a mind uses to read a city

Kevin Lynch found that people hold a city in their head as paths, edges, districts, nodes and landmarks. At tile scale each one becomes one deliberate move. Christopher Alexander's patterns (small squares, a promenade, a four-storey limit, lit windows) become the generator's rules.

2 · Simulation

Schedule is the truth, motion is a view

Each person is one row in typed arrays, about 64 bytes: home, workplace or school, role, age, one of twelve day plans, a few minutes of jitter, a trait and three friends. Where anyone is at time t is a lookup in their plan plus a cached street route, so knowing where all 1,000 people are costs almost nothing, and someone who walks off-screen comes back exactly where they should be. Saves hold the seed, the clock and what changed.

Tiers by distance. A walker promotes to a full agent at the same spot on the same route, and demotes by handing their place back to the plan.

3 · Information

Bells, clocks, windows and banners

The Ranch speaks in wood, bells and painted signs. The City is its big sister and adds a public clock, shop windows, chalkboards, lamps and bunting. The question every time: how would a townsperson know this?

Chronograph complications are what a townsperson glances at the tower for: the next bell, market day, school in or out, the festival countdown and the carts. The world suggests one when it matters, such as the festival dial the day before. The census and ledger never become complications.

4 · Assets

Six heroes, one kit, a grammar, and the Ranch's models

Tiers from the image-to-scene granularity rubric. Kit modules sit on a 3 m storey and a 4.5 m bay.

Heroes: town hall with clock tower, market hall, the café (the Morning Café's home), bakery, school, fountain and well.

Kit: townhouse ground floors (door, three shopfronts, arch), upper floors (two windows, balcony, bay), roofs (gable, hip, dormer), chimney, corner, stair, awnings, sign bracket, lamp post, bollard, stall, cart, bunting, laundry line, gate, wall, bridge.

Grammar: streets and kerbs from the street graph, Townscaper-style stacking, window lights, bunting, tree rows, house colours from the figure band. No new models.

People: one body in four shapes and three ages, twelve role silhouettes.

5 · The board

It needs its neighbours

Each edge is a road stub. A neighbour turns it into a route with a real cart on it.

A three-hex card. In the hand it's a bloom-tier card like the Moonbase: The City, "A town of a thousand neighbours", with coin, heart and hammer icons (all in the Card Forge already). Dragging it shows the three-hex ghost from the existing footprint code, which already tries all six rotations.

Adjacency you can see. Each neighbouring food tile fills a row of market stalls. A city with no neighbours still works, but its square looks emptier. Nobody fails.

One signal. The City listens to the same time, season, weather and mood as the Ranch, so the whole board goes to dusk together.

6 · Plan

Phases

Recommendation

Approve the market town: three hexes, 1,000 real people, schedule as truth. Start the crowd proof now.

The crowd proof is the riskiest part and it is initiative 1: 1,000 scheduled people on the existing three-hex footprint, gated at 30 fps on Evan's iPhone and 1.5 ms of simulation. The concept sheets run alongside it. When the town generator lands, the City takes Futurama's slot in the tray, and Futurama becomes a retro-future style variant.

Considered and set aside: a one-hex city (needs towers); a symbolic city of a few dozen walkers and a counter (the number would be a lie); full agents for all 1,000 (about ten times the phone's CPU budget).

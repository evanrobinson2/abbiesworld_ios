Over-the-Shoulder Play

How proper third-person games frame the camera, dodge walls, drop you from a map into a body, and make that body feel alive, and what Abbie's World should take from them. Nothing here is built yet.

The dive

One unbroken move, about 1.2 seconds, anchored on the child you picked. The camera keeps looking down until the last third, then lifts its eyes to the horizon. At the bottom she glances back at you, then faces the goat she has noticed. No cut, no fade, no words.

What the good ones share

A soft shoulder

True over-the-shoulder is for aiming, and we have no guns. Centre the camera while she runs; slide to the shoulder when she stops, so what she faces gets the open half.

Fast in, slow out

A small sphere cast finds walls and snaps the camera in front of them, then eases back. Posts, fences, leaves and goats never push it; they thin to a soft dither for the player only.

One curve, one anchor

Height, pitch, lens and look-ahead all ride one dial. Detail swaps in while the camera moves, where the eye forgives it. Pinch out and the same curve takes you back to the exact board view.

A body that notices

AAA uses motion matching on hours of capture. We get most of the feel from a few clips and cheap layers on top. Her head turning to the bleating sheep tells you more than any marker.

Five options for Abbie's World

A
camera

Soft shoulder, automatic

The camera follows, recentres behind her and leans toward bells, gates and animals. One thumb anywhere walks; a second finger looks around. Nothing on screen until a thumb lands.

B
transition

The dive

Tap a character and fall into them in one move. She glances back to say "it's me now". Pinch out to rise back to the board.

C
animation

A living body

The upgraded player animation: speed-matched walk and run, lean, turn-steps, a head that looks, springy ears and scarves, and a body that shivers, hurries or yawns with the weather and time.

D
interaction

Touch the world

Near things warm softly. Tap one and she walks over and does it, hand reaching the real thing: pet the goat, pick the apple, ring the bell. No prompts and no words.

E
idea only

Ride along

For "play my day": the camera follows a character through their own day; a thumb takes the wheel and you "run around"; let go and they carry on.

Start with A and B together (one camera system), then C, then D. E waits.

Cheap on a phone

Only the player gets a skeleton: one skinned model, a few thousand triangles, at most three clips blending. Everyone else keeps today's light motion. The camera does three short casts against simple boxes, never the real meshes. Together the camera and body should cost under half a millisecond a frame.

Lessons taken from Manor Lords, Valheim, Enshrouded, Sable, Fall Guys, Bluey, PAW Patrol World, State of Decay 2 and The Alters (all in Evan's library), plus Mario Odyssey, Breath of the Wild, Sky, Astro Bot, A Short Hike, God of War, Resident Evil 4 and Journey. Style yes, expression never: ideas and techniques, never their characters, clips, footage or camera data.

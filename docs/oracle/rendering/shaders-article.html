Shaders: what they don't teach you in 3D school

3D school teaches you to model, texture and light a thing so it looks real. Games that look wonderful mostly break those rules on purpose. Here are nine of the rules they break, each one running live below so you can switch it on and off.

Drag any scene to turn it. Everything is plain three.js, the same engine Abbie's World runs on.

First, the question

Had the Game Oracle indexed shaders?

No. Before today, the Oracle knew 379 of Evan's games, and for most of them it had one sentence about the art ("Chunky pixel-painterly kingdoms on a floating board") plus Steam's own style tags on about a third of them (realistic, pixel art, cartoon). Nothing said how a game is actually shaded.

So the Oracle now has a rendering dimension on every studied game: how light falls (stepped bands, soft wrap, painted in, flat or physical), where the outlines come from, what the surfaces are made of, which screen effects sell the look, and the one trick that makes it. 62 games were studied by hand, 306 were read from their art notes at low confidence, and 11 still need a play session. Each game's Oracle page now has a "How it's drawn" section.

What 368 games say

Almost nobody uses outlines. Everybody uses air.

Three things stand out. Only 19 of the 368 games draw any kind of outline. Only 12 use the classic stepped "cel" light. And Evan's five favourites (Tiny Glade, Dorfromantik, Besiege, Bricktales and 9 Kings) use none of either. Their look comes from soft light, clean colour, contact shadows and a little air. That is the strongest finding here, and it shapes the toon set.

Rule 1

Shadows are a colour, not black

3D school darkens a surface as it turns from the light. Painters never do that. A shadow on a red barn on a sunny day is a cool, slightly purple red, because the blue sky is lighting it. Mix toward a cool tint instead of toward black and the scene stops looking dirty.

vec3 shadow = base * mix(vec3(0.18), skyTint, hueShift);
vec3 lit    = base * sunColour;
vec3 colour = mix(shadow, lit, light);

Seen in: Team Fortress 2, Tiny Glade, Fall Guys, Dredge.

In Abbie's World: every toon look tints its shadows from the sky colour, so when the sky turns dusk-orange, every shadow follows.

Rule 2

The light ramp is the art director

Real light falls off smoothly. A toon shader takes that smooth number and pushes it through a ramp: two bands, three, or a soft curve. Team Fortress 2's well-known trick is to wrap the light first (half of it reaches round the back, so nothing goes fully dark) and then look the result up in a hand-painted strip. Change the strip and the whole game changes mood, without touching a single model.

Seen in: Team Fortress 2 (soft ramp), Borderlands and Marvel Rivals (hard bands), Thronefall (faceted, two tones).

In Abbie's World: the ramp is a handful of numbers per place. The Ranch gets soft three-band light; the Moonbase gets two hard bands.

Rule 3

Rim light draws the silhouette

Kids read shapes by their outline. A thin bright edge where a surface turns away from the camera separates every character from the background, even in a busy scene. It costs one line of shader code: the more a surface faces sideways to the camera, the brighter its edge.

float rim = pow(1.0 - dot(normal, toCamera), 3.0);
colour += rimColour * rim * rimStrength;

Seen in: Team Fortress 2, Fall Guys, Disney Dreamlight Valley, the Storybook study.

Rule 4

Outlines are geometry, not a filter

The cheapest good outline is a second copy of the model, pushed out a little along its normals, drawn inside-out in ink colour. Only its rim shows, so it looks like a line. Two things school never mentions. The shell needs smooth normals even when the model is faceted, or the corners crack open. And the width should shrink with distance, like a pen line in perspective; lines that stay the same on screen make far-off things look scribbled.

Seen in: Marvel Rivals and Dispatch (shell outlines), Borderlands (lines painted into textures plus an edge pass), Sable (lines carry everything, almost no shading).

In Abbie's World: outlines are an option, not the default, because Evan's five favourites don't use them. Ink Toon is the look for the school, the brute fight and anything comic-shaped.

Rule 5

Normals are a brush

A normal is the direction a surface faces, and the shader lights it by that direction. Nothing forces the normal to match the real shape. Give a lumpy tree crown the normals of one big sphere and it lights like a single soft ball of leaves, the way an illustrator paints it, instead of a pile of separate blobs. Stylised faces in animated films use the same trick to keep cheeks and noses from catching ugly shadows.

Seen in: stylised foliage in most toon games, Disney Dreamlight Valley, anime-style character shading.

In Abbie's World: the modeler's build.py recipes can write a "lighting shape" for bushes, trees and hair, kept beside the real mesh.

Rule 6

Grounding beats shadows

School spends weeks on cast shadows. For a toy-sized world, the shadow that matters is the dark smudge right under each thing, where it touches the ground. Without it, objects float. With it, a sharp sun shadow is almost optional. Townscaper and Tiny Glade lean on this: deep soft darkness in corners and at contact points.

Seen in: Townscaper, Tiny Glade, Bad North, Lego Bricktales.

Rule 7

Air is a shader too

Fog in school is a performance trick to hide the far plane. In stylised games it's the mood. Bad North's islands sit in soft fog; Dredge turns its fog cold and wrong at night; the Storybook study's green haze is what makes the garden feel warm. The fog colour should come from the sky, and distant things should fade toward it, never toward grey.

In Abbie's World: fog is in-world air, so it's diegetic: characters can feel a mist rolling in. Nothing fades at the screen edges; the world itself gets hazier.

Rule 8

Low resolution is a feeling

Valheim renders rough low-poly models with modern light and air, and it's beautiful. Mouthwashing renders at a tiny size on purpose, with wobbling pixels, and it's unsettling. Fewer pixels is a choice with a feeling attached: memory, nostalgia, unease. Grace's 8-bit basement and Daddy's gold coins live here.

Seen in: Valheim, Mouthwashing, Kingdom Two Crowns, Dave the Diver (pixel sprites over 3D).

Rule 9

One signal drives every number

Here is the rule that matters most, and it's already Abbie's World canon: the whole game adapts. A toon shader is just a handful of numbers (sun direction and colour, shadow tint, rim colour, sky, fog). Drive all of them from one game signal, such as time of day, a storm coming or a mood, and the world changes together. Against the Storm recolours its whole map for the storm season; Gris gives colour back as the story heals.

In Abbie's World: the same signal also drives music, sound and the animals (sheep heading home at dusk), per GAME_FEEL.md.

The toon set Evan asked for

Seven looks, one shader

The new toon set isn't built yet, so here it is as a proposal. Every look is the same shader with different numbers, so switching is free and one game signal can blend between them. Try each one in the scene at the top of the page. The Storybook study (AW-STUDY-0028) is the eighth: the full post-processed film look, for hero moments and cutscenes.

Cost on a phone: light means a few extra lines in the one material; medium adds a second draw of each model (outlines) or a screen-space pattern. None of the seven needs post-processing, unlike the Storybook look.

Relation to the Storybook study

Storybook is the film; the toon set is the game

AW-STUDY-0028 showed that three.js can match a soft animated-film frame: soft wrapped light, rim light, glowing skin at the shadow edge, ambient occlusion, green haze, lens blur, glow and a colour grade. Its heavy parts (lens blur, glow, occlusion) are post-processing passes that cost real frame time on a phone. The toon set keeps the cheap half of that study inside one material: the wrap, the rim, the skin glow (Clay), the tinted shadows and the air. A scene can play in Sunday Toon and switch on the Storybook passes for a cutscene. Open the Storybook study.

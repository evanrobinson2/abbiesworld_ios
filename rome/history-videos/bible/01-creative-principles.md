# Creative Principles

Status: **LOCKED.** These are not open for renegotiation. Every frame card is
checked against them at Phase 9.

---

## 1. Continuity extends beyond characters

The audience should subconsciously learn the world. Marcianople must be *our*
Marcianople. Once the eastern gate, the headquarters courtyard, the banquet
building and the road outside are established, later frames may not rearrange them
because the model found a prettier composition.

Locations are built like sets: wide reference, reverse angle, ground plan,
architecture and materials, time-of-day variants. That is what makes it possible to
direct movement through space.

## 2. Physical causality persists

AI imagery resets reality between images. Ours does not.

If it rains, the road stays muddy. If a wagon loses a wheel, that wagon does not
reappear intact. If the Goths have been starving for two weeks, their bodies show
it. If a warrior takes a Roman shield at Marcianople, it is his shield from then on.
If a cloak tears, it is repaired, not regenerated.

The work accumulates history on objects and bodies. See `05-continuity-and-state.md`.

## 3. Scale is a storytelling instrument

Alternate radically between the enormous and the intimate.

Thousands along the Danube, then a filthy hand breaking the last bread between two
children. An army crossing Thrace, then a soldier stopping to shake a stone from his
sandal. The oscillation is what stops the audience converting people into unit
counters.

## 4. Ordinary life is mandatory

Raw, but not dystopian.

People joke. Children play. Someone flirts. A soldier eats something and likes it.
A woman argues her goat back. Two retainers laugh at something Fritigern said. Roman
soldiers gamble while waiting. Someone sleeps in the sun. A child is transfixed by a
helmet.

This is what makes the later catastrophe hurt. If everyone is miserable from the
first minute, misery becomes wallpaper.

> **Rule: human beings remain human when history isn't happening to them.**

## 5. No villains by rendering

Lupicinus does not get sinister shadows and a malevolent face because we know what
he is going to do. He is a Roman commander handling a frightening situation badly
and perhaps corruptly. At the banquet he may be genuinely convivial for fifteen
minutes. That makes the decision that follows far more disturbing, because it looks
like something a person did rather than something the designated villain was always
going to do.

Fritigern is not visually sanctified either. He is intelligent and compelling, and
he later conducts a brutal war. We do not tell the audience whom to like.

In drawn and painted registers this risk shifts from lighting to draftsmanship:
**no character may be rendered with an uglier, heavier, or more caricatured line
than any other.** Image models caricature villains reflexively; this must be
actively suppressed in every prompt.

## 6. Information has geography

The story turns repeatedly on people not knowing things. That is excellent drama.

When the skirmish begins outside Marcianople, we do not cut outside to show it. We
stay inside with Lupicinus. Something is heard. Perhaps nothing. Then a messenger
arrives out of breath.

At Adrianople, we do not cut to the Gothic cavalry so the audience knows where it
is. If Valens does not know, we do not know. Horses. Dust. Something is happening.

In this medium the reveal is controlled by **hold length and cut timing**, since
there is no page turn. Withheld information is a scheduling decision on the beat
board, and it must be marked there explicitly.

## 7. The frame has ethics

Sometimes it stays. Sometimes it turns away. Sometimes it watches something horrible
from 150 metres because that is plausibly where our observer is. Sometimes we are
inside it.

The frame does not automatically go to the most spectacular vantage. A massacre seen
through a doorway past hiding civilians may say more than a perfect wide shot of
three hundred men dying.

## 8. Maps belong to the same world

No generic animated-history arrows. Maps are built from tactile late-antique
geography — topography, rivers, roads, settlements — beautifully restrained. Where
the history is uncertain, movement is a broad zone, not a suspiciously precise arrow.

A map must answer a question: Where are they? Why can't Rome contain them? Where is
Gratian? Why does Valens think he has time? Never "here is another map because
documentaries have maps."

(Deferred to the Danube phase, but the rule stands from the start.)

## 9. Visual hierarchy of evidence

The viewer learns the grammar without being told. Implemented concretely as the
tiered style registers in `02-style-bible.md`:

- **Confident** — history is solid; the frame is fully realised and we go inside it.
- **Reconstructing** — plausible but inferred; rendering loosens, the frame becomes
  more observational.
- **Unknown** — history genuinely fails; the image cannot go there, and says so.

Marcianople gives the first and best example: we may stay outside the banquet room
for the crucial missing seconds. Movement. Someone shuts a door. Then we cut to
what Ammianus actually reports happened next.

This is an artistic language derived directly from historiographical honesty.

## 10. Never illustrate the narration

If the narration says *"Thousands of Goths gathered on the northern bank of the
Danube,"* we do not necessarily show thousands of Goths on a riverbank. Perhaps we
are watching a Roman sentry stare across the water.

If it says *"They asked Rome for permission to cross,"* perhaps we see two soaked
Gothic emissaries standing in an immaculate Roman administrative room.

Narration supplies information. The image supplies experience. When the two
duplicate each other you get an AI slideshow. When they complement each other you
get drama.

## The stack

```
Historical truth
  → Human experience
    → Visual world
      → Character continuity
        → Material continuity
          → Spatial continuity
            → Unique frame direction
              → Edit
```

## The two tests

1. **The grey-sweat test.** Does this look like a real place with real weather,
   real dirt, and real bodies in it?
2. **The overriding test.** With the narration removed, is this people living
   through something, or illustrations of something that happened?

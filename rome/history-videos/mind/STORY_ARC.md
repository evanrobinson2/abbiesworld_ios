# Canonical story arc — Fritigern to Alaric

Status: **current fictional dramatic continuity**

This document is the shared narrative context for the Rome/Gothic project. It is a
historically rooted dramatic retelling, not a claim that every relationship or event
below is attested. Keep the distinction explicit:

- **Historical spine**: Gothic migration into the Roman Empire, exploitation and famine,
  Marcianople, revolt, Adrianople, Valens's death, later Alaric and the sack of Rome.
- **Fictional connective tissue**: Fritigern's family relationships, the wedding attack,
  Alaric as Fritigern's son, the identity of the steppe horsemen, the deliberate banquet
  reversal, Fritigern's wound/illness, and other intimate scenes used to make the history
  emotionally legible.

## Part I — Young Fritigern: the uniter

Fritigern begins as a young man before he is a war leader. He is practical, rooted,
reluctant to seek power, and gifted at bringing people together.

Two Gothic peoples remain culturally distinct:

- **Fritigern's side** — the wanderers: mobile, displaced, survival-shaped.
- **Her side** — the steppe horsemen: proud horse culture, powerful cavalry tradition,
  more rooted in the old tribal order.

Fritigern falls in love across that divide. The marriage is not a cynical alliance first;
it is love first, with political consequence following from it. Their wedding becomes the
living symbol that the two peoples can become one.

### The wedding wound

The wedding is attacked.

In the chaos, each faction instinctively pulls its own loved ones toward safety. Fritigern's
people rescue the bride and groom. The parents/old generation are killed.

The wedding therefore does three things at once:

1. love unites the two peoples;
2. violence destroys the generation that previously held them apart;
3. the surviving couple becomes the last living bridge between the groups.

Fritigern does not seek leadership, but responsibility falls onto him. The two peoples end
this movement as **wanderers together**.

The steppe horsemen then disappear from the foreground of the story as a distinct force.
The audience is allowed to forget them.

## Part II — Alaric is born from the union

In this fictional continuity, Fritigern and his wife have a son: **Alaric**.

Alaric is therefore not merely Fritigern's child. He is literally the child of the union:

- son of the wanderers;
- son of the steppe horsemen;
- born from a marriage that joined two peoples;
- heir to both the love that united them and the violence that destroyed the old order.

The audience initially believes the larger story is about Fritigern. The deeper structure is
that the first half is showing **how Alaric was made**.

## Part III — Flight, Rome, and humiliation

Pressure from the Huns and wider instability destroys ordinary Gothic life. The united
people move because they must, not because they are seeking conquest.

Fritigern leads them toward Rome because Rome represents order, food, land, protection,
and survival. They arrive as supplicants.

Rome fails them.

Food becomes scarce and is exploited. Families are humiliated. Weapons are surrendered.
People who came asking for protection are treated as disposable. Children experience the
consequences directly.

Young Alaric witnesses this world.

The central emotional object is **bread**.

> They beg for bread. They will not beg again.

The humiliation is intimate rather than abstract: hands asking for food, children hungry,
parents unable to provide, warriors surrendering the things they had sworn to use to protect
their families.

## Part IV — Marcianople: the apparent defeat and the reversal

Fritigern is wounded around Marcianople in the dramatic retelling. The wound persists and
becomes a physical inheritance marker for Alaric: the child sees the scar and knows what
Rome did to his father and his people.

The Goths appear helpless, but the film gradually reveals that desperation was mistaken for
weakness. Training flashbacks or inserted scenes show discipline, signals, horsemanship,
weapons practice, trust, and coordination.

### The banquet

Fritigern enters the Roman banquet knowing betrayal is possible. His retinue appears exposed.
The Romans believe they control the situation.

In this fictional version, **Fritigern anticipated the betrayal**.

The commotion outside the gate is deliberately instigated by Goths to provoke a predictable
Roman response. For a moment, the audience believes the retinue knowingly walked into death
and perhaps sacrificed themselves.

Then comes the reversal:

**They knew all along.**

The retinue is prepared. The Roman guards respond exactly as expected. The Roman commander /
Caesar figure is the one who looks surprised. The people treated as beggars are revealed as
experienced soldiers capable of shaping events.

Fritigern escapes. Humiliation turns into organized revolt.

## Part V — Open revolt and Adrianople

The Gothic resistance expands across Thrace. Fritigern remains the reluctant unifier rather
than becoming a conqueror caricature.

At Adrianople in 378, Valens's army is destroyed.

Historically, Gothic cavalry returning to the battlefield is a decisive part of the Roman
catastrophe. In the fictional family architecture, that cavalry is given an emotional
identity:

**the steppe horsemen return.**

They are the bride's people — the half of the family that disappeared from the foreground
after the wedding.

The audience has almost forgotten them. Then: horses, dust, recognition.

Their return works simultaneously as:

- a truthful historical battle mechanism: cavalry return changes Adrianople;
- a narrative callback to the wedding and lost steppe culture;
- the return of the missing half of Alaric's family;
- proof that the original union survived displacement.

Young Alaric witnesses the return of his mother's people.

In the dramatic retelling, Valens flees into a rural farmhouse/estate structure and Fritigern
ultimately makes the decision that seals his fate. This is fictionalized around the historical
uncertainty of Valens's death.

## Part VI — Fritigern fades

Victory does not turn Fritigern into an emperor figure. He is injured, exhausted, mortal,
and still fundamentally the man who wanted his people to live.

Historical Fritigern disappears from the record after this period. In the fictional
continuity, his old wound and later illness gradually remove him from the story.

His disappearance is therefore emotional rather than merely historiographical.

Alaric inherits:

- his father's scar and memory;
- his mother's steppe lineage;
- the humiliation of Roman dependency;
- the experience of hunger;
- the memory of surrendered weapons;
- the proof that divided peoples can become one.

## Part VII — the hidden protagonist becomes visible

Alaric grows into the leader the audience knows from later history.

He deals again with Rome through negotiation, settlement, broken promises, resistance, and
eventually force. The story does not frame his later actions as simple revenge. The long
objective remains what the people sought at the beginning:

**land, food, security, dignity, continuity, and the ability to feed their own children.**

The audience finally understands that the apparent Fritigern story was also an Alaric origin
story.

Fritigern was the uniter.
Alaric is the inheritor.

## Final movement — Rome leaves the frame

At the end, imperial spectacle recedes. We return to ordinary life: wagons, families,
mothers, children, food.

The final image is intimate:

**a child is being fed inside a wagon.**

That image answers the central wound.

The people who were forced to beg for bread can feed their children by their own hands again.

## Emotional spine

**Love → union → massacre → wandering → refuge → humiliation → hunger → apparent defeat →
reversal → revolt → cavalry return → inheritance → self-possession.**

## Core sentence

> **They begged for bread. They will not beg again.**

## Structural reveal

The audience thinks it is watching **Fritigern's story**.

It is actually watching **the making of Alaric**.

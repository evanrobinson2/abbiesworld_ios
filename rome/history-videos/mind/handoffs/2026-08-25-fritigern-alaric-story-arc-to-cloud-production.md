## Status and historical boundary

This is a **fictionalized motion-picture continuity built on a historical spine**.
Do not silently present invented relationships or events as established history.

Historical spine:

- Fritigern led Goths during the crisis of 376–378 and at Adrianople.
- Valens died at Adrianople in 378, probably after taking refuge in a farm building
  that was burned.
- Alaric was probably born around 370, so he could plausibly be a small child during
  the crossing and approximately eight at Adrianople.
- Fritigern disappears from the surviving record after this period.
- Alaric later became the dominant Gothic leader and sacked Rome in 410.

Creative continuity invented or deliberately compressed for the film:

- Fritigern is Alaric's father.
- Young Fritigern begins as a reluctant farmer/protector who becomes a unifier.
- The Marcianople banquet disturbance is a Gothic operation Fritigern anticipated.
- Fritigern is wounded at Marcianople and carries the wound through the later war.
- Fritigern personally makes the final decision that causes Valens's death in the
  burning farmhouse.
- Fritigern's disappearance and Alaric's later career are shaped into one
  father-to-son inheritance arc.

## Core logline

A young Fritigern wants land, family, and ordinary life, but becomes the man capable
of uniting a displaced people. Rome admits them as refugees, strips them of weapons,
starves and humiliates them, and teaches Fritigern's young son Alaric what it means to
beg for bread. Fritigern turns apparent helplessness into organized resistance,
defeats an emperor, and leaves Alaric the wound, memory, and unfinished purpose that
will carry the story all the way back to Rome.

The movie is initially presented as Fritigern's story. It is gradually revealed to be
**the story of how Alaric was made**.

## Part I — Young Fritigern: uniter of people

- Begin before catastrophe, with ordinary Gothic life: land, crops, livestock,
  children, courtship, work, argument, humor, and communal music.
- Fritigern is not born wanting conquest or kingship. He wants to protect a household
  and work good land.
- The Gothic world is politically fragmented. Under pressure, young Fritigern proves
  able to reconcile families, warriors, and rival groups around common survival.
- His defining gift is not savagery; it is **making many people act as one people**.
- Establish the ancestral humming motif here. It begins with a few human voices and
  will eventually belong to the whole migrating society.
- Establish the visual motifs before they become painful: bread freely shared,
  weapons used as protection, wagons as homes, horses as ordinary working animals,
  children watching adults.

## Part II — The world comes apart

- Hunnic pressure and wider collapse force the Goths from their land.
- Fritigern does not choose migration for glory. He chooses it because remaining means
  destruction.
- He leads families, not merely soldiers, toward the Danube and the Roman promise of
  refuge, food, land, and lawful settlement.
- Alaric is now a small child inside Fritigern's household. At the crossing he is
  roughly six; at Adrianople roughly eight, within the uncertainty of his birth date.
- Fritigern's political success in uniting people now becomes a burden: every child,
  wagon, animal, and hungry family is his responsibility.

## Part III — Refuge becomes humiliation

- Rome receives the Goths as a manageable population rather than as human beings.
- They surrender weapons they had sworn to use to protect their families.
- Food is withheld and traded through corruption. People are reduced to pleading,
  bargaining, and losing family members over the most basic need.
- Child Alaric watches his father kneel before Roman power.
- He sees people close to him taken; in the emotional continuity, his best friend is
  stolen over food and bread.
- Concrete image pair: **Roman boot + tied hand**.
- Central motif: bread changes from ordinary nourishment into the measure of power,
  dignity, and dependence.
- The audience should believe the Goths have been broken. Underneath, Fritigern is
  observing, organizing, and preparing.

## Part IV — Marcianople: the apparent trap reverses

- Lupicinus invites Fritigern and the Gothic leadership to the banquet while violence
  gathers outside.
- The audience initially reads the banquet as another humiliation and an obvious Roman
  trap: why would starving refugees accept food from the men abusing them?
- Reversal: **Fritigern knew.** The commotion outside the gate was deliberately
  instigated by the Goths to provoke the predictable Roman response.
- The retinue enters knowing what may happen. Their apparent vulnerability is part of
  an operation, not passive despair.
- Avoid framing this as suicide. It is disciplined risk and sacrifice with a fallback
  revolt/escape plan.
- Training flashbacks reveal that the people presented as beggars were experienced,
  coordinated soldiers all along.
- The guards attack, but the Goths are ready. The Roman commander is the one caught
  off guard: **"the Caesar is the one that looks surprised."**
- Fritigern escapes and turns the attempted decapitation into open resistance.
- During the fighting at Marcianople, Fritigern suffers a serious wound. He survives,
  but the wound becomes the bodily history Alaric will later remember.

## Part V — Revolt and Adrianople

- The same people who begged permission to live now move through Thrace as an army
  Rome cannot contain.
- Fritigern remains a reluctant farmer-warrior rather than becoming a conventional
  conqueror. His strategic purpose is survival, leverage, food, land, and a durable
  settlement.
- Valens misreads the situation and commits before support arrives.
- Information geography matters: the audience should not know more than Valens about
  the missing Gothic cavalry until the reversal strikes.
- The Roman army collapses at Adrianople.
- Valens flees to a rural Thracian farm building. In this telling, Fritigern reaches
  the final moral threshold and orders—or directly causes—the building to burn.
- Valens's death is not a victory quip. It is the moment the abused refugee leader
  chooses an irreversible act and becomes part of the violence he resisted.

## Part VI — The father fades; the son inherits

- Fritigern survives the victory but cannot simply return to who he was.
- The Marcianople wound persists. Earlier conversation also proposed cancer as an
  explanation for his disappearance; **cause of decline remains an open creative
  decision unless Evan settles it**.
- He recedes rather than crowning himself. The reluctant unifier has carried his people
  through the crisis but cannot complete the settlement personally.
- Alaric grows up seeing the scar, hearing the story, and remembering the boot, tied
  hand, surrendered weapons, stolen friend, and bread.
- The inheritance is not "hate Rome." It is a vow that his people will never again be
  made dependent enough to beg for survival.

## Part VII — Alaric: the hidden protagonist becomes visible

- Alaric's later negotiations, rebellions, and march on Rome are the long consequence
  of the childhood wound.
- He repeatedly seeks terms, land, recognition, food security, and a legitimate place
  for his people. Rome repeatedly delays, uses, divides, or betrays them.
- The sack of Rome is not framed as barbarian appetite. It is the terrible culmination
  of a relationship Rome helped create.
- Fritigern united the people and taught them they could resist. Alaric carries that
  unity into a second generation and forces Rome to confront what it made.
- The film's emotional revelation is complete: Fritigern drove the historical action,
  but Alaric was the child absorbing its meaning.

## Ending — bread restored

- Remove Rome from the frame.
- Return to wagons, families, land, food, and ordinary human life.
- End at intimate scale, not with a triumphal army or burning city.
- A parent feeds a child inside a wagon or new settlement.
- The food is provided by their own people, with no Roman official between the child
  and the bread.
- Closing thought/line:

> They crossed the river. They begged for bread. **They will not beg again.**

The final image answers the first wound. The victory is not conquest; it is
**self-possession**.

## Recurring cinematic language

- **Bread:** home → scarcity → humiliation → restored agency.
- **The scar/wound:** history carried on Fritigern's body and inherited by Alaric.
- **Weapons:** protection → forced surrender → recovered capacity.
- **Hands:** sharing food, tied hands, kneeling, training signals, feeding a child.
- **Wagons:** ordinary home → migration → defensive formation → settled domestic life.
- **Horses:** daily life planted early → military reversal → riderless imperial horse.
- **Watching child:** Alaric is present before the audience understands why.
- **Human humming:** the Gothic identity motif, growing from household to whole people.

## The central breakthrough

Do **not** prompt Suno primarily with prose about mood, history, or cinematic intention.
That produces too much freedom and tends to collapse toward generic soundtrack language.

Give Suno a **musical chassis first**, then decorate it.

Working order:

> **tempo → meter → subdivision/feel → drum placement → bass behavior → vocal phrasing →
> instrumentation → arrangement arc → exclusions**

The prompt should read like dense production shorthand, not a treatment explaining why the
music matters.

Story, scene progression, and detailed narrative instructions belong in the lyrics/section
structure when possible; the Style prompt should describe what the music physically does.

---

## How Evan learned to hear the chassis

The useful breakthrough came from reducing rhythm to mouth-feel instead of notation.
Traditional notation and tablature were too abstract for the live creative process.

Evan's own mnemonic for swing became approximately:

> **BOOOOM-t BOOOOM-t**

The sustained word carries most of the subdivision; the final `t` is almost only a
percussive event. This made the long-short asymmetry of swing physically intelligible.

For straight 4/4, the reference feel was closer to:

> **BOOM — TAH — BOOM — TAH**

The important conceptual separation is:

- **pulse** = the unwavering clock;
- **subdivision** = straight vs swung/lopsided spacing inside the clock;
- **groove** = where bass, vocals, and other sounds sit around that clock.

This became the control language for Suno.

## Reference-track lesson

The Peter's Barque reference (`I Want to Want`) was useful because the underlying rhythm is
simple and stable while the interesting movement happens around it.

**Correct measured reference tempo: 103.4 BPM.**

The previous ~92 BPM note was wrong and has already been corrected elsewhere in the hive.
Do not reintroduce it.

The lesson is not “copy this song.” The lesson is:

- straight 4/4;
- simple grounded backbeat;
- stable pulse;
- sparse percussion;
- syncopated bass/drone movement around the pulse;
- conversational vocal phrases that begin before/between strong beats or cross bar lines.

Complexity should come from **phrasing and layering**, not from making the drum pattern busy.

## The Steppe prompt method that finally worked

The original prompt was too much of a creative brief: long prose about the ancient steppe,
family, migration, instrumentation, emotional trajectory, etc. The correction was to put the
musical instructions first and compress them into descriptors Suno could act on.

A representative working Style-prompt shape was:

> **~103 BPM, straight 4/4, hypnotic primitive folk groove, deep skin-drum kick on 1 and 3,
> dry frame-drum strike on 2 and 4, sparse percussion, syncopated low bass/drone moving
> around a rigid pulse. Ancient Eastern European steppe folk atmosphere; raw handmade
> acoustic textures: skin drums, frame drum, jaw harp, bowed lyre, horsehead-fiddle-like
> strings, rough wooden percussion, only occasional bone flute. Prominent low male humming
> and wordless communal humming; low intimate male narrative vocal, melodic spoken-singing,
> syncopated conversational phrasing, call-and-response. Sparse opening; recurring ancestral
> motif; intensity grows through layered voices and humming rather than more instruments.
> Earthy, intimate, human, ominous but not warlike. No modern drums, synths,
> fantasy-medieval clichés, or cinematic trailer production.**

This is a template, not a sacred exact prompt. Keep the hierarchy.

### Important corrections from iteration

- **Less flute.** Too much flute made the output feel generic and over-melodic.
- **Humming is gold.** Evan strongly responded to low communal humming; it feels bodily,
  ancestral, migratory, and human. Make humming a recurring identity device.
- The **humming can carry the Gothic motif** instead of assigning that motif to flute.
- Do not solve intensity by adding more instruments. Grow through voices, density,
  harmonics, stomps, drones, and repeated motifs.
- Avoid "barbarian battle music," "Viking," generic medieval fantasy, and trailer-score
  language unless deliberately desired for a specific departure.

## Why the tracks can feel like one world without being one song

Evan wants multiple pieces to blend or feel genetically related while still allowing major
emotional departures.

Do not force every track to have one BPM.

Measured current music:

- `dust-on-the-steppe`: **112.3 BPM, E♭ minor**
- `frozen-plain-thrace`: **107.7 BPM, E♭ major**
- Peter's Barque reference: **103.4 BPM**

The stronger continuity device may be **E♭ as tonal center plus recurring rhythmic/vocal
DNA**, with mode and tempo allowed to change for dramatic function.

Useful invariants across related tracks can include:

- same or neighboring tonic;
- recurring low humming motif;
- similar straight 4/4 physical pulse;
- same family of skin/frame/wood percussion;
- related bass/drone vocabulary;
- recurring call-and-response behavior;
- common sonic exclusions (no glossy trailer orchestra, no generic fantasy medieval).

Variable dimensions can then carry story:

- tempo;
- major/minor mode;
- density;
- vocal population;
- amount of distortion/electric material;
- rhythmic aggression;
- silence/headroom.

This lets the score evolve rather than merely repeat.

## Modern elements: use them surgically

Evan does **not** want historical-purity cosplay. Modern timbres can coexist with the steppe
world when they serve the emotional mechanics.

Prior successful direction included:

- modern electric **slap bass** underneath ancient/handmade percussion;
- occasional **wah-wah guitar** punctuation;
- no strong modern melody instrument taking over;
- keep the old-world texture in drums, breath, strings, humming, and communal voices.

The principle is **fusion through role**, not genre mashup. A modern instrument should do a
specific job in the groove rather than announce “now this is funk/rock/techno.”

Note: during the reversal discussion Evan explicitly rejected making the new cue simply
“techno Gothic.” Do not revive that as the default direction.

## Vocals and lyrics are structural

For dense historical storytelling, the lead vocal should behave like narration that happens
to be musical:

- low/intimate delivery;
- melodic spoken-word / sung narration;
- conversational phrasing;
- syncopation around the beat;
- phrases crossing bar lines;
- occasional held syllables rather than every word landing on a beat.

Then use **call-and-response** to enlarge the social scale:

1. individual male voice;
2. men answering;
3. women joining separately;
4. mothers/children or full community response.

The chorus/refrain matters. Earlier generations felt compelling but lacked a memorable
return point. A recurring sung/hummed motif or lyrical refrain should give the audience a
place to come home to.

For this story, recurring textual/musical material can orbit the bread/continuity motif,
but do not mechanically turn every emotional idea into lyrics.

## Arrangement doctrine

**Do not begin epic. Earn epic.**

A useful steppe movement arc:

> sparse human opening → settled groove → additional voices → unease → pressure → communal
> expansion → movement

A useful reversal/battle arc can depart from that chassis, but the reveal should feel earned:

> apparent vulnerability → tension → withheld information → attack → reversal → recognition
> of competence → propulsion

Training flashbacks are important because they retroactively explain the reversal. Music can
participate by exposing fragments of the same rhythmic/motif vocabulary earlier in quieter
form, then revealing their full aggressive form at the reversal.

At Adrianople, the cavalry return should have musical ancestry from the wedding/steppe
horsemen material. It is not merely “battle gets louder”; it is the **return of the lost half
of the family**.

## Prompting protocol for Luna (Music)

When Evan asks for a Suno prompt:

1. Ask/derive the **function of the cue** in the story, not merely its mood.
2. Identify what musical DNA must remain continuous with adjacent cues.
3. Put **BPM/meter/groove** first.
4. Specify percussion physically (which beat gets what), but keep it simple unless complexity
   is intentionally desired.
5. Specify bass/drone behavior separately from drums.
6. Specify vocal behavior separately from instrumentation.
7. Use a short prioritized instrument list; do not give Suno a museum inventory.
8. State the arrangement arc in compact language.
9. Finish with exclusions that prevent the most likely failure modes.
10. Keep Style prompts compact enough that every descriptor has a job. Do not turn them into
    essays.

Then iterate **one or two variables at a time**. If a generation has the right groove, do not
rewrite the whole prompt. Preserve the chassis and change the offending dimension (e.g.
“less flute,” “more humming,” “wah-wah actually audible,” “stronger refrain”).

---

## The deeper creative rule

Evan does not need to become fluent in notation to direct music. The successful interface is
**embodied language → musical parameter → generation**.

Examples:

- “BOOOOM-t” → swung subdivision
- “BOOM — TAH” → straight grounded pulse
- “the `t` is only a sound” → make the offbeat short rather than equal-duration
- “the humming is RAD” → promote communal humming to identity/motif
- “too much flute” → reduce a melodic timbre without disturbing the groove

Luna (Music) should translate Evan's physical/verbal descriptions into production parameters
without forcing him through music-theory vocabulary first. Teach vocabulary only when it
helps him control the result.

## Success test

A prompt is good when Evan can hear the generated track and say what to change in **one
musical dimension** rather than having to say “this isn't it.”

The music system is working when the prompts give him a controllable Lego set:

> rhythm + instrumentation + vocal behavior + motif + story function + arrangement.

That is how the tracks begin to work together while still being allowed to evolve.
